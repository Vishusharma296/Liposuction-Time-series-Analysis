# Problem Statement

## 1. Background and Practical Need

Water-jet-assisted liposuction (WAL) removes subcutaneous adipose tissue using a suction cannula combined with a pulsed water jet. During the procedure, the interaction between cannula, water jet, vacuum, and tissue produces a mechanical response that is perceived by the operator as resistance and haptic feedback. For the development of liposuction devices, validation of surgical handling, and training purposes, realistic test materials are therefore needed that reproduce not only the geometry of adipose tissue but also its mechanical response under representative WAL conditions.

Human subcutaneous adipose tissue is the most direct reference material, but its systematic use in engineering experiments is limited by availability, ethical requirements, biological variability, storage and handling constraints, and the difficulty of obtaining repeatable measurements from the same specimen. Artificial tissue phantoms are a practical alternative because their dimensions and material composition can be controlled. Existing concepts include water-gelatin and water-agar systems, including heterogeneous structures intended to imitate the non-uniform internal architecture of adipose tissue. Their usefulness, however, depends on whether they can be shown quantitatively to behave like human adipose tissue during liposuction. Visual similarity or subjective handling alone is not sufficient evidence of mechanical fidelity.

The central engineering need is therefore a standardized, sensor-based methodology that can compare artificial fat-tissue phantoms with human adipose tissue under liposuction-relevant loading and quantify the degree of similarity. Such a method should be application-specific: it must evaluate the tissue during interaction with the actual WAL cannula system rather than relying exclusively on conventional bulk material tests that do not reproduce the loading conditions of liposuction.

## 2. Scientific and Experimental Problem

Previous work at the Chair of Microfluidics demonstrated that axial force signals can be recorded from a modified Human Med *body-jet* cannula system and that different test materials produce distinguishable force-time responses. The earlier experiments compared human abdominal adipose tissue with three phantom types: a 12% (m/m) water-gelatin matrix containing 2% agar hemispheres, a 2% (m/m) water-agar phantom, and a 4% (m/m) water-agar phantom. The results also showed that water-jet and vacuum settings influence the measured signal.

This established technical feasibility, but it did not yet provide a robust measure of tissue-phantom fidelity. The previous procedure relied on manual cannula movement and manual specimen fixation. Although the operator attempted to maintain a constant insertion cadence and predominantly axial motion, the trajectory and velocity were not mechanically constrained or instrumented. The measured force signal therefore contains both material response and operator-dependent motion. A difference between two recordings cannot automatically be attributed to tissue mechanics.

The existing data also contain further sources of variability. Force baselines shift between sessions because of changes in sensor-adapter preload. Recordings were acquired at approximately 10 Hz and 100 Hz, have substantially different durations, and do not contain balanced coverage of WAL operating conditions. In the currently audited dataset, most human recordings were obtained with water and vacuum active, whereas only a minority of the phantom recordings were collected under the same condition. A pooled classifier could therefore learn machine configuration, sampling regime, operator, or session characteristics instead of material behavior. High classification accuracy alone would consequently not prove that the phantom and human tissues are mechanically different for the intended scientific reason.

The data also have a nested structure. Multiple recordings from the same phantom specimen or human tissue source are repeated measurements, not independent material or biological replicates. The effective sample size is therefore much smaller than the number of files or signal windows. Splitting one recording into many segments and treating these as independent samples would create pseudoreplication and overstate model performance. The research problem is thus a small-sample, repeated-measures signal-comparison problem in which standardization, confound control, and physically meaningful signal analysis are more important than maximizing algorithmic complexity.

## 3. Research Gap

The research gap is the absence of a validated workflow that combines **standardized mechanical execution, sensor-based tissue characterization, and quantitative tissue-phantom similarity assessment**.

First, the experimental procedure requires improved mechanical standardization. The thesis task calls for the design, manufacture, and evaluation of a modular test facility for standardized acquisition of liposuction data under defined conditions. Guided translational motion and controlled specimen fixation should reduce uncontrolled variation in cannula trajectory and specimen movement and improve repeatability. The cannula translation remains manually actuated, while its motion is restricted to a single translational degree of freedom. Penetration depth is controlled by limiting the travel of the linear carriage to a predefined length, whereas cannula velocity is not mechanically controlled and remains operator-dependent. The purpose of the test setup is not to reproduce every motion of clinical liposuction, but to create a more controlled loading condition in which measured differences can be attributed more confidently to the tested material.

Second, the sensor signals require a processing strategy that preserves mechanically relevant differences while accounting for measurement artifacts. Baseline shifts must be corrected at recording level. Sampling-rate harmonization must respect the information content of the lowest-rate data: 10 Hz recordings cannot be made equivalent to 100 Hz data by simple interpolation. Signal duration must also be treated in physical time rather than by truncating all recordings to an equal number of samples. These issues must be resolved before different tissues can be compared fairly.

Third, phantom fidelity is fundamentally a similarity problem, not merely a classification problem. Classification can show whether groups are separable, but it does not by itself determine which phantom most closely resembles human tissue. A defensible evaluation must quantify the distance between the mechanical responses of human and artificial tissue under matched or otherwise controlled conditions and identify the signal characteristics responsible for the remaining differences. Relevant descriptors may include force magnitude and variability, loading rate, energy, frequency-domain content, and temporal complexity. The planned similarity analysis includes Maximum Mean Discrepancy and/or Wasserstein distance, while Random Forest and XGBoost are planned as secondary machine-learning methods for differentiation and feature-importance analysis. These models should remain secondary to the similarity assessment and must be validated at the level of independent subjects or specimens.

## 4. Research Aim and Scope

The aim of this master's thesis is **to develop and evaluate a standardized, data-based methodology for the mechanical characterization of artificial fat-tissue phantoms during water-jet-assisted liposuction and to quantify their similarity to real human adipose tissue**.

The work therefore combines experimental-system development with data analysis. A modular test setup will be designed and manufactured to constrain the cannula to a single translational degree of freedom and define specimen positioning while retaining manual cannula actuation. Penetration depth is controlled by restricting the linear-carriage travel to a predefined length, while cannula velocity remains operator-dependent. Artificial fat-tissue phantoms based on suitable hydrogel systems, including agar- and gelatin-based materials, will be produced and tested alongside available human adipose-tissue reference data. The final phantom dataset will comprise eight distinct formulations. The sensor-enhanced WAL system will record the mechanical response, with axial force as the principal measured quantity in the existing setup. The resulting signals will be quality-checked, corrected for recording-specific offsets, and processed so that comparisons remain meaningful despite differences in sampling frequency and recording duration.

The analytical objective is to determine which signal properties vary with phantom composition, whether different formulations can be distinguished reliably, and which formulation is mechanically closest to the human reference under comparable WAL conditions. Similarity should be expressed as a quantitative proximity or fidelity measure based on physically interpretable signal features and/or spectral characteristics, with uncertainty reported explicitly. Classification may be used as a complementary test of separability, but not as the sole evidence of fidelity. The analysis must preserve the experimental grouping structure so that repeated measurements from one specimen do not masquerade as independent samples.

A further objective is to evaluate whether increased mechanical standardization improves the repeatability of the recorded force response. This directly links the test-bench design to the validity of the phantom comparison: if the measurement process is not repeatable, a data-driven ranking of materials cannot be considered reliable. Where sufficient formulations are available, the study will also examine the relationship between phantom composition, measured mechanical response, and similarity to human adipose tissue, providing a rational basis for selecting or refining future phantom formulations.

The expected contribution is therefore a reproducible experimental and analytical framework rather than a single high-performing classifier. A successful outcome will provide a standardized WAL test procedure, a defensible signal-processing and quality-control workflow, a quantitative method for differentiating and ranking artificial fat-tissue phantoms against human tissue, and an explicit treatment of uncertainty and remaining limitations. This framework can support the future development of more realistic training phantoms while reducing dependence on scarce human tissue and on material tests that do not reproduce the mechanical conditions of liposuction.
